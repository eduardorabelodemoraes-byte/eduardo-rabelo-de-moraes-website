LOCALIZATION · TRANSCREATION · GLOBAL MARKETS

Speaking every language is not the same as understanding every market

As multilingual content scales, the strategic question is no longer only how much can be translated, but where cultural specificity belongs.

By Eduardo Rabelo de Moraes · 24 November 2026 · 10 min read

Topics
Localization strategy
Marketing transcreation
Cultural relevance

Leia em português →

In February 2025, Duolingo killed its green owl.

The announcement was part of Dead Duo, a global campaign in which the company turned the apparent death of its mascot into a mystery, a social-media narrative and, eventually, a challenge for users to bring him back by completing lessons.

Duo died almost everywhere. But not in Japan.

During an earnings call later that month, Duolingo’s CEO, Luis von Ahn, explained that the company had staged the mascot’s death in every market except Japan, where joking about death required greater caution. Germany moved in another direction: the local story became darker and more theatrical, turning Duo’s resurrection into a cult-inspired narrative. The campaign remained recognisable, but its central premise did not travel unchanged.

This was not a failure of global consistency. It was a clearer definition of what consistency actually required.

The character remained Duo.
The route through which his personality reached different audiences did not have to remain the same.

That distinction — between distributing an idea and understanding how it should arrive — sits at the heart of multilingual growth.

When reach grows faster than understanding

In November 2026, during the Q&A at Local Trust, Global Growth, I raised a question about how linguistic quality translates into local trust, and how that trust influences consumer decisions.

The conversation, presented by MultiLingual Media with support from Smartling, examined the relationship between international expansion, localization and sustainable growth. A few days later, the Translating Europe Forum approached the changing language industry from another direction: the value language professionals add through judgment, accountability, clarity and participation in workflows increasingly shaped by technology.

The two events left me with a related question:

What happens when multilingual scale grows faster than market understanding?

Producing content in more languages has become faster. Companies can distribute campaigns, product interfaces and customer communication across markets with a reach that would once have demanded far more time and resources.

Availability is not the same as relevance.

A message may be grammatically correct, terminologically consistent and technically functional while still sounding distant, misplaced or unconvincing. A brand may be visible in a market without being recognisable within it. It may even use the right language while making the wrong assumptions about the people reading it.

Scale does not automatically make cultural specificity more important in every piece of content. A safety instruction, a contractual clause and a product slogan do not require the same degree of adaptation. What scale changes is the number and consequence of the choices that must be made about them.

A system designed to vary

Spotify Wrapped provides a useful example because it is simultaneously a product experience, a data narrative and a global marketing event.

In 2023, Spotify reported that Wrapped was available in 170 markets and more than 35 languages. Its global marketing team described the work involved in building a scalable design system that could function across those languages while still accounting for market nuances and the difference between global and local execution.

The important word is not simply global. It is system.

Wrapped needs a recognisable visual identity, a consistent product structure and a shared promise: to transform listening data into a personal story. Those elements allow millions of users to recognise the experience as the same annual event.

But a scalable system cannot assume that every phrase, layout, artist reference or public activation will carry the same meaning everywhere. In 2025, the global campaign extended into approximately 50 physical experiences across more than 30 markets. One of them placed a giant paw on Copacabana Beach in Rio de Janeiro to celebrate Lady Gaga — a local expression inside a global creative architecture.

Scale is not creating every market from scratch.
Nor is it forcing every market into an identical execution.

It means designing a structure firm enough to remain recognisable and flexible enough to accommodate meaningful variation.

This is where localization begins to look less like a final production stage and more like part of communication design.

The slogan stays. The rap changes.

Some content requires a different level of intervention.

Bosch built its Like a Bosch campaign around a pun on the English expression like a boss. The slogan is compact, recognisable and closely tied to the brand name. Replacing it could weaken the central device; translating it literally would destroy it.

So the slogan remained in English.

The accompanying rap could not.

Its humour, product references, rhyme and rhythm had to be reworked for different languages while still fitting the pace and musical structure of the campaign. French, Spanish and Vietnamese versions required more than the replacement of words. In Switzerland, the adaptation was developed line by line with a local rapper, testing what sounded natural and what would produce the intended effect.

The result illustrates a principle that is easy to miss when localization is discussed only in terms of output volume: different elements of the same campaign may require different degrees of freedom.

The slogan remained global.

The rap became local.

The brand identity depended on both choices.

That is not inconsistency. It is proportional adaptation.

Cultural specificity is not cultural decoration

The opposite mistake is also possible.

If insufficient adaptation can make communication feel remote, excessive adaptation can make it feel artificial. Adding local symbols, idioms or visual references does not automatically demonstrate cultural understanding. Sometimes it reveals how little understanding informed the choice.

Research into localized brand communication offers an important warning. Across four studies, consumers evaluated global brands less favourably when their communications incorporated highly symbolic local cultural elements rather than less symbolically charged ones. Other research has associated certain forms of cultural mixing with a perception of cultural intrusion.

Culture is not a decorative layer.

A recognisable monument, traditional garment, religious symbol or familiar expression may appear to offer an immediate route to local relevance. But highly visible cultural borrowing can feel reductive, opportunistic or invasive when the relationship between the brand and the symbol has not been earned.

Cultural specificity therefore depends on more than the number of local elements added to a campaign. It requires knowing which differences matter, when they matter and how visibly they should shape the message.

Adapt too little and the communication may remain foreign to its audience. Adapt too visibly and culture may be reduced to performance. Adapt through generalisation and entire markets become stereotypes.

The difficult work lies between those outcomes.

What one string can reveal

The same problem can appear at a much smaller scale.

While contributing to the European Portuguese localization of Foundation, a medieval city-building game developed by Polymorph Games, I encountered a source string with a limit of 25 characters.

Source string Surplus of Drinking Goods
Possible Excesso de Bebidas
Selected Excedente de Bebidas

Both alternatives could be defended linguistically. Only one accurately represented the system behind the words.

Understanding the distinction required looking beyond the isolated string and examining the game’s production, consumption and resource-management mechanics. Drinks in Foundation are produced, stored, sold and consumed through the tavern economy. The source was not describing excessive drinking or an undesirable quantity. It referred to resources remaining above current demand.

Within that system, excedente was more precise than excesso.

A screenshot could have shown where the string appeared. It might have revealed its relationship with a menu, resource panel or interface label. But location on a screen is not the same as function within a game.

Visual context

Where is this string used?

Functional context

What is it doing inside the system?

Linguistic choice

Which wording preserves that function?

That difference matters far beyond games. A sentence inside a campaign, interface or customer journey belongs to a structure of actions, expectations and consequences. Translating the visible words without understanding that structure may produce language that looks correct and behaves incorrectly.

Trust is not a language deliverable

This brings us back to local trust.

Linguistic and cultural adequacy can support trust. Poor terminology, unnatural phrasing, inappropriate humour and careless cultural references can certainly undermine it.

But localization cannot manufacture trust independently.

Trust also depends on product quality, price, customer service, privacy practices, institutional behaviour and whether a company fulfils the promises its communication makes. A polished local message cannot compensate indefinitely for an experience that contradicts it.

Language helps determine whether a brand’s conduct and intentions can be understood, recognised and evaluated by a particular audience.

That is already a significant responsibility.

Localization can make a promise intelligible. Transcreation can preserve its intended effect. Neither can guarantee that the promise deserves belief.

Where the decision belongs

Not every text needs transcreation. Not every interface needs cultural reinvention. Not every difference between markets needs to be made visible.

The strategic value of language expertise lies partly in identifying those distinctions.

Some content needs consistent multilingual availability. Some requires linguistic, cultural and functional adaptation to a market. Some communication — particularly slogans, campaigns, humour and brand storytelling — may need to be recreated around its intended effect. Personalization introduces another level, adapting experiences to segments or individuals, often through data that raises separate questions about privacy, inference and manipulation.

These are not interchangeable tasks.

Treating them as one undifferentiated localization process encourages two opposite failures: spending creative effort where consistency would serve the audience better, or applying standardisation where meaning depends on local expectations.

The answer is not to place a cultural reviewer at the end of every workflow. By then, the architecture may already have determined what can be changed, which assets are available, how much space the language may occupy and which assumptions have been built into the product.

Cultural and linguistic judgment becomes more useful when it can influence those choices earlier: what must remain fixed, what may vary, what requires local research and what should not be adapted at all.

Technology can expand multilingual production. It can identify patterns, generate alternatives and make context more accessible. What it cannot settle as a universal rule is how much cultural difference a particular message should preserve, reveal or adapt.

That is not a contest between people and machines. It is a question of where responsibility enters the workflow.

Beyond speaking

Speaking every language is not the same as understanding every market.

But understanding a market does not mean covering a global campaign with familiar local symbols, nor assuming that everyone within a country responds to the same references. Markets contain regions, generations, communities and individuals whose expectations do not fit neatly inside a single cultural profile.

Understanding begins with a more modest form of attention: knowing what must be researched, what cannot be assumed and which choices require someone to examine the relationship between language, purpose and consequence.

Dead Duo did not need to die in Japan for the campaign to remain global. Spotify Wrapped does not need every execution to look identical in order to remain recognisable. Like a Bosch could preserve its slogan while rewriting the language surrounding it. And a single word in a game interface could change once the system behind it became visible.

Each case points to the same conclusion.

Multilingual scale is not only a question of producing more language.
It is a question of designing where difference belongs.

And the more communication expands, the more carefully that choice must be made.
